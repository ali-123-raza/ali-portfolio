The Evolution of Set Theory

From Cantor’s infinite worlds to the frontiers of modern mathematics: how a nineteenth-century investigation into infinity transformed the foundations of mathematics and opened an extraordinary landscape of research.

By Ali Raza

The mathematics behind all mathematics

What is mathematics ultimately about? Numbers, equations, geometric figures, functions, spaces and structures appear to belong to different mathematical worlds. Yet beneath these seemingly different objects lies a remarkably unified language: the language of sets.

Today, the real number system can be constructed using sets. Functions can be represented as sets of ordered pairs. Algebraic structures, topological spaces and even sophisticated mathematical theories can be formalised within set theory.

But this was not always the case.

The emergence of set theory during the nineteenth century transformed mathematics in ways that few mathematicians could have anticipated. What began as an investigation into the properties of real numbers and infinite collections eventually became a comprehensive framework for mathematical reasoning.

More remarkably, set theory did not simply provide mathematics with a convenient language. It forced mathematicians to reconsider some of their most fundamental assumptions.

The questions set theory raised

  1. Are all infinities equal?
  2. Can an infinite collection be larger than another infinite collection?
  3. Does every mathematically describable collection constitute a set?
  4. Can mathematics be completely formalised through axioms?
  5. Are there mathematical truths that cannot be proved from our accepted axioms?

These questions shaped the development of modern mathematical logic and continue to influence research today. The history of set theory is therefore not merely a history of definitions and theorems. It is a story of intellectual ambition, controversy, foundational crises and some of the most profound discoveries in mathematics.

Before set theory: the long journey toward infinity

The mathematical investigation of collections and infinity is considerably older than set theory itself.

Ancient Greek philosophers, particularly Zeno of Elea, raised fundamental questions about infinity, continuity and divisibility. His famous paradoxes challenged the intuitive understanding of motion and the infinite divisibility of space.

Aristotle subsequently distinguished between potential infinity and actual infinity. Potential infinity describes a process that can continue indefinitely, such as counting the natural numbers, whereas actual infinity treats an infinite totality as a completed mathematical object. This distinction remained important throughout the development of mathematics.

During the nineteenth century, mathematicians increasingly encountered infinite collections in analysis, geometry and algebra. The development of rigorous real analysis created a particular need to understand infinite collections of numbers and points. Several mathematicians contributed to this transition.

Bernard Bolzano (1781–1848) defended the mathematical legitimacy of actual infinity. His posthumously published Paradoxes of the Infinite (1851) examined the unusual properties of infinite collections and anticipated several ideas that later became central to set theory.

Bernhard Riemann (1826–1866) contributed to the development of analysis and geometry, creating an intellectual environment in which the study of collections of points became increasingly important.

Richard Dedekind (1831–1916) developed a systematic set-theoretic approach to mathematical structures. His work on algebraic number theory, the real numbers and the foundations of arithmetic helped establish the importance of sets and mappings in rigorous mathematics.

These developments prepared the ground for a mathematician whose work would fundamentally change the understanding of infinity.

Georg Cantor and the birth of modern set theory

No historical account of set theory can begin without Georg Cantor (1845–1918). Born in St. Petersburg and later working in Germany, Cantor initially pursued problems in analysis, particularly those concerning trigonometric series and the representation of functions. His investigations led him to study sets of points and, eventually, the extraordinary properties of infinite collections.

In 1874, Cantor published a landmark paper demonstrating that the real algebraic numbers can be enumerated, whereas the real numbers as a whole cannot be placed in one-to-one correspondence with the natural numbers. This discovery established a fundamental distinction between different sizes of infinity.

The discovery of different infinities

Before Cantor, infinity was often treated as a single undifferentiated concept. Cantor demonstrated that infinite sets could have genuinely different cardinalities. Two sets have the same cardinality if there exists a bijection between them. For example, consider

\[\mathbb N=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad E=\{2,4,6,8,\ldots\}.\]

Although the second set appears to contain only half of the natural numbers, the function \(f(n)=2n\) establishes a bijection between them. Thus

\[|\mathbb N|=|E|=\aleph_0.\]

This illustrates one of the most surprising properties of infinity: an infinite set can have the same cardinality as one of its proper subsets.

Cantor went much further. Through his diagonal argument, he demonstrated that the real numbers are uncountable:

\[|\mathbb R|>|\mathbb N|.\]

This was a revolutionary result. Infinity was no longer merely an endless process; it had become an object of mathematical comparison and investigation.

The transfinite world

Cantor introduced cardinal numbers to measure the sizes of sets and ordinal numbers to describe the order types of well-ordered sets. The finite world of ordinary arithmetic was now accompanied by a theory of transfinite numbers, with an elaborate hierarchy of infinite cardinalities and ordinal structures.

Cantor’s work also led to the famous Continuum Hypothesis (CH), which asks whether there exists a cardinality strictly between that of the natural numbers and that of the real numbers:

\[2^{\aleph_0}=\aleph_1\,?\]

The problem would become one of the most influential questions in the history of mathematical logic.

Infinity was no longer merely an endless process; it had become an object of mathematical comparison.

The personalities who shaped set theory

Set theory developed through the contributions of several remarkable mathematicians. Some extended Cantor’s ideas, some challenged their philosophical foundations, and others transformed the subject through entirely new methods.

Richard Dedekind: the architect of mathematical structure

Dedekind was one of the earliest mathematicians to recognise the foundational importance of sets and mappings. His Was sind und was sollen die Zahlen? (1888), translated as The Nature and Meaning of Numbers, presented a systematic construction of the natural numbers and clarified the role of infinity, mathematical induction and recursive definitions. His contributions helped establish the structural and logical approach that became characteristic of modern mathematics.

Leopold Kronecker: the constructivist critic

Kronecker (1823–1891) was among the prominent critics of Cantor’s approach to infinity. He emphasised the importance of constructive procedures and questioned the legitimacy of certain abstract existence assumptions. His disagreements with Cantor reflected a much deeper philosophical debate: should mathematics accept abstract infinite objects, or should mathematical existence be tied to explicit construction? This tension between constructive and non-constructive approaches remains relevant in the foundations of mathematics.

Gottlob Frege and Bertrand Russell: logic and the foundations

Frege (1848–1925) attempted to establish arithmetic on a rigorous logical foundation. His work profoundly influenced the development of mathematical logic, although his foundational system encountered a devastating contradiction.

Bertrand Russell (1872–1970) independently discovered a paradox within naive set theory in 1901. Consider the collection of all sets that are not members of themselves, \(R=\{x : x\notin x\}\), and ask whether \(R\) belongs to itself.

This became known as Russell’s Paradox. It exposed a serious weakness in the unrestricted principle that every definable property determines a set, and forced mathematicians to reconsider the foundations of set theory.

Ernst Zermelo: the axiomatic revolution

Ernst Zermelo (1871–1953) played a decisive role in establishing the modern axiomatic approach to set theory. In 1904 he proved the Well-Ordering Theorem, which states that every set can be well-ordered. His proof relied on the Axiom of Choice and generated considerable discussion.

In 1908, Zermelo published his axiomatisation of set theory. Rather than allowing arbitrary collections to form sets, his system specified principles governing which sets could exist and how new sets could be constructed. This was a major turning point: set theory was transformed from an informal theory of collections into a formal axiomatic discipline.

Abraham Fraenkel, Thoralf Skolem and John von Neumann

The axiomatic framework continued to develop. Abraham Fraenkel (1891–1965) and Thoralf Skolem (1887–1963) contributed to the refinement of Zermelo’s system, particularly through the formal treatment of the Separation and Replacement principles. John von Neumann (1903–1957) contributed the modern treatment of ordinals and the Axiom of Foundation.

These developments led to the standard framework known as Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory with the Axiom of Choice, \(\mathrm{ZFC}\), which became the most widely adopted foundational system for mainstream mathematics.

Kurt Gödel: the limits of mathematical foundations

Kurt Gödel (1906–1978) fundamentally changed our understanding of mathematical proof. His incompleteness theorems, published in 1931, demonstrated that sufficiently expressive and consistent formal systems cannot establish every mathematical truth expressible within them.

His contributions to set theory were equally remarkable. In 1938, Gödel introduced the constructible universe, denoted \(L\), and demonstrated that the Axiom of Choice and the Generalized Continuum Hypothesis are consistent with ZF, provided ZF itself is consistent.

Paul Cohen: the discovery of forcing

Paul Cohen (1934–2007) completed one of the most remarkable chapters in the history of the Continuum Hypothesis. In 1963 he developed the method of forcing and demonstrated that the negation of the Continuum Hypothesis is consistent with ZFC, assuming ZFC is consistent.

Gödel and Cohen together

Assuming ZFC is consistent, \[\mathrm{ZFC}\nvdash\mathrm{CH}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathrm{ZFC}\nvdash\neg\mathrm{CH}.\] The Continuum Hypothesis is independent of ZFC.

This discovery did more than settle a longstanding problem. It introduced a powerful technique that became central to modern set theory.

The axiomatic foundation: understanding ZFC

The development of axiomatic set theory represented a fundamental change in mathematical method. Instead of assuming that every conceivable collection constitutes a set, mathematicians introduced carefully formulated axioms governing set existence and construction. The ZFC system consists of the Zermelo–Fraenkel axioms together with the Axiom of Choice.

The axioms of ZFC, in plain words

Extensionality
Two sets are equal if they have exactly the same elements.
Empty set
There exists a set containing no elements.
Pairing
Any two sets can be placed together in a set.
Union
The union of a set of sets exists.
Power set
Every set has a set consisting of all its subsets.
Infinity
An infinite set exists.
Separation
Subsets can be formed by restricting an existing set to elements satisfying a specified property.
Replacement
The image of a set under a definable function is also a set.
Foundation
Membership never descends through an infinite chain.
Choice
A choice function exists for every set of non-empty sets.

These axioms provide a systematic foundation for constructing mathematical objects. An important feature of ZFC is its ability to formalise essentially all ordinary mathematical structures: natural numbers, integers, rational and real numbers, functions, groups and topological spaces can all be represented within its framework.

Nevertheless, ZFC is not the only possible foundation. Alternatives include von Neumann–Bernays–Gödel set theory (NBG), Morse–Kelley set theory (MK) and other foundational frameworks.

The great unresolved question: the Continuum Hypothesis

The Continuum Hypothesis remains one of the most fascinating problems in mathematical logic. Cantor asked whether the cardinality of the real numbers is the very next infinite cardinal after that of the natural numbers; in modern notation, whether \(2^{\aleph_0}=\aleph_1\).

For decades, mathematicians attempted to establish this statement or its negation. Gödel’s constructible universe showed that CH could not be disproved from ZFC, assuming consistency. Cohen’s forcing method then showed that CH could not be proved from ZFC, under the same assumption. The result is extraordinary: the conventional axioms of set theory do not determine the answer.

The open question

Should mathematicians accept additional axioms capable of deciding the Continuum Hypothesis, or is its independence a sign that there is no uniquely determined answer within our accepted foundations?

This question connects mathematical logic with philosophy, the nature of mathematical truth and the search for new foundational principles.

The emergence of modern set theory

By the middle of the twentieth century, set theory had evolved far beyond its original investigation of infinite collections. It had become a sophisticated research discipline with several interconnected branches.

Cardinal and ordinal arithmetic

The study of infinite cardinal and ordinal numbers remains central to set theory. Cardinal arithmetic investigates the sizes of infinite sets and operations such as \(\kappa+\lambda\), \(\kappa\cdot\lambda\) and \(\kappa^\lambda\). Ordinal arithmetic studies the structure and ordering of well-ordered sets. Both reveal that infinite arithmetic behaves in fundamentally different ways from finite arithmetic.

Large cardinal theory

Large cardinal theory investigates increasingly strong forms of infinity: inaccessible, Mahlo, weakly compact, measurable, strong, supercompact and huge cardinals, among others. These notions form a hierarchy of increasingly strong set-theoretic existence assumptions.

Large cardinals matter not merely because they describe enormous infinite objects, but because they provide powerful tools for measuring the consistency strength and structural properties of mathematical theories. They also play an important role in descriptive set theory, inner model theory and the search for new axioms.

Forcing and independence results

Forcing is among the most influential techniques in modern set theory. It allows mathematicians to construct extensions of mathematical universes in which new sets exist, while carefully controlling which properties remain true. It has been used to establish independence results involving the Continuum Hypothesis, the Axiom of Choice, Suslin’s Hypothesis, statements of cardinal arithmetic and combinatorial principles about infinite sets.

Forcing also led to forcing axioms, including Martin’s Axiom, the Proper Forcing Axiom (PFA) and Martin’s Maximum (MM), which provide powerful frameworks for investigating the structure of the set-theoretic universe.

Descriptive set theory

Descriptive set theory investigates definable sets, particularly subsets of Polish spaces such as the real line. It studies the complexity of sets that arise through operations involving open sets, complements and countable unions. Its central notions include Borel sets, analytic and coanalytic sets, projective sets, definable equivalence relations and determinacy principles. The subject has significant connections with topology, real analysis, measure theory, dynamical systems and logic.

Inner model theory

Inner model theory investigates canonical inner models of the set-theoretic universe. Gödel’s constructible universe \(L\) was the starting point. Later work, particularly by Ronald Jensen and others, led to sophisticated fine-structure methods and to inner models associated with large cardinal hypotheses. These models help mathematicians understand the internal structure of the universe of sets and compare the consistency strength of competing axioms.

Infinite combinatorics

Infinite combinatorics extends familiar combinatorial ideas into the infinite setting. Its central topics include partition relations, trees and tree properties, stationary and club sets, cardinal characteristics of the continuum, almost disjoint families, ultrafilters and infinite Ramsey theory. These ideas connect set theory with combinatorics, topology, algebra and other branches of mathematics.

Set theory today: a living research discipline

It would be misleading to regard set theory as a subject whose major achievements ended with Gödel and Cohen. Contemporary set theory is an active field, increasingly focused on the relationships between different axiomatic assumptions and how they shape the structure of mathematical objects. Some important directions:

  • The search for new axioms. Should ZFC be supplemented with additional principles? Large cardinal axioms and forcing axioms are the main candidates, and researchers study their consequences, consistency strengths and relationships with one another.
  • Large cardinals and forcing axioms. PFA, Martin’s Maximum and related principles have deep consequences for cardinal arithmetic, infinite combinatorics and the structure of the real line.
  • Inner model theory and core model induction. Canonical models capturing ever stronger large cardinal properties, closely tied to consistency strength, determinacy and definable sets.
  • Descriptive set theory and Borel combinatorics. Classifying mathematical structures through definable equivalence relations, with applications to group theory, ergodic theory, dynamical systems and topology.
  • Set theory and the rest of mathematics. Its techniques increasingly contribute to topology, functional analysis, combinatorics, group theory and the classification of structures.

A useful indication of the field’s current direction is the January 2025 Set Theory workshop at the Mathematisches Forschungsinstitut Oberwolfach. It brought together researchers working on forcing, large cardinals, inner models, infinitary combinatorics, descriptive set theory and Borel combinatorics, and its report highlighted new connections between these areas. Modern set theory is not a collection of isolated theories; it is a highly interconnected discipline whose techniques and foundational questions continue to evolve.

Why set theory still matters

The significance of set theory extends well beyond the study of infinity. Its foundational role provides a common language for mathematical structures, and its techniques have produced powerful results in topology, analysis, combinatorics and logic. Perhaps its greatest contribution, however, is the way it has changed our understanding of mathematical knowledge itself. Set theory teaches us that:

  • Infinite collections can have different cardinalities.
  • Mathematical existence requires careful foundational principles.
  • Some mathematical propositions are independent of familiar axiomatic systems.
  • Stronger axioms can produce richer mathematical universes.
  • Mathematical research is not limited to proving statements from an established list of assumptions; it also investigates the assumptions themselves.

In this sense, set theory is both a foundation of mathematics and a field in which some of mathematics’ deepest philosophical and logical questions are investigated.

The continuing story of infinity

From Bolzano’s reflections on infinity to Cantor’s transfinite mathematics, from Zermelo’s axiomatisation to Gödel’s constructible universe and Cohen’s forcing, the development of set theory is one of the most remarkable intellectual journeys in mathematics. What began as an investigation into collections of numbers and points became a comprehensive theory of infinity and a central foundation for modern mathematics.

Yet the story is far from complete. Questions about large cardinals, the Continuum Hypothesis, forcing axioms, inner models and the structure of the set-theoretic universe continue to challenge researchers. Perhaps this is what makes set theory so extraordinary: after more than a century, the mathematical universe still contains questions whose answers lie beyond our present axioms.

The study of infinity, it seems, has no finite ending.
